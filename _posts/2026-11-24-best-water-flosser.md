A toothbrush can't reach the tight spaces between your teeth, which is why dental organizations such as the ADA recommend cleaning between your teeth once a day. String floss works, but plenty of people find it fiddly and skip it. A water flosser (also called an oral irrigator) sprays a thin, pulsing stream of water along the gumline and between teeth, and many people find it easier to stick with.

For this guide we compared the 2026 water flosser roundups from Popular Science and NBC Select, then checked what the research says about how well water flossers work. We didn't test these products ourselves; the findings below are attributed to the reviewers who did.

## Does a water flosser actually work?

Short answer: it can help, but don't expect miracles. A Cochrane systematic review of home interdental cleaning devices found low- to very-low-certainty evidence that using an oral irrigator alongside brushing may reduce gum inflammation (gingivitis) in the short term compared with brushing alone, and the authors described the overall evidence for irrigators as limited and inconsistent. They did not find a clear effect on plaque.

In practice, the best between-teeth cleaner is the one you'll actually use every day. If string floss is a struggle, or you have braces, bridges or implants that make flossing awkward, a water flosser is a reasonable option. Many people use one in addition to floss or interdental brushes rather than instead of them. Your dentist or hygienist can tell you what fits your mouth best.

## How to choose

**Countertop vs. cordless.** Countertop models like the Aquarius have bigger tanks and more pressure settings, so you can finish without refilling. Cordless models are smaller, work in the shower and travel well, but their tanks run out faster. If you have the counter space and an outlet near the sink, countertop is the easier everyday choice.

**Pressure range.** More settings matter mostly at the low end. Start on the lowest setting, especially if your gums bleed easily or you're new to water flossing, and increase it gradually. A little bleeding in the first days can happen; if it continues, check with your dentist.

**Reservoir size.** Look at how many seconds of flossing a full tank gives. Around 60 to 90 seconds lets you do the whole mouth in one go.

**Tips.** Most flossers come with a standard jet tip. Orthodontic tips (for braces) and tips designed for implants or periodontal pockets are useful if they apply to you. Plan to replace tips periodically, as the manufacturer recommends.

**Cleaning.** Water tanks can grow mildew. Empty the reservoir after each use and let it air-dry. Removable or dishwasher-safe parts make this much easier.

**ADA Seal.** Some water flossers carry the ADA Seal of Acceptance. It's a helpful extra signal, though products without it can still be effective.

## How to use a water flosser

- Fill with lukewarm water and start on the lowest pressure.
- Lean over the sink and close your lips around the tip so water doesn't spray everywhere.
- Aim the tip at the gumline and pause briefly between each tooth, working from the back teeth forward on both the outer and inner surfaces.
- Keep brushing twice a day for two minutes with fluoride toothpaste; a water flosser adds to brushing, it doesn't replace it.
