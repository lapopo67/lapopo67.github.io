Getting a child to brush for a full two minutes, twice a day, can feel like a nightly negotiation. A good kids' electric toothbrush won't do the job for them, but a built-in timer, a gentle head sized for small mouths and a bit of fun can make the routine much easier for everyone.

For this guide we compared the current kids' toothbrush roundups from CNN Underscored, whose 2026 guide had two young testers use each brush for weeks under parental supervision, and Live Science, which also tests with children and consults dental experts. We checked the brushing basics against guidance from the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) on HealthyChildren.org and the American Dental Association (ADA) Seal of Acceptance listings. We didn't test these products ourselves; findings are attributed to the publications that did.

One thing every source agrees on: the brush matters less than the habit and the help. Even the best electric toothbrush works best with an adult supervising and checking technique.

## How to choose

**Match the brush to your child's age.** The AAP says children usually need help brushing until about age 8. Experts quoted by Live Science say many kids can start using their own electric toothbrush from about age three with an adult supervising, and a pediatric dentist cited by CNN Underscored doesn't recommend electric brushes for kids under three. For babies and toddlers, a soft manual brush that you use for them is fine.

**Look for a timer and quadrant prompts.** CNN calls a two-minute timer with 30-second alerts one of the most important features, since it helps kids clean the whole mouth evenly. Not every kids' brush has one, so check before you buy.

**Soft bristles and a small head.** The AAP recommends a brush made for young children with soft bristles and a small head. Several reviewers found some heads and handles too large for younger kids, so consider the size of your child's mouth and hands.

**Rechargeable or battery.** Battery brushes cost less up front and are handy for travel, but replacement batteries add up. Rechargeable models usually cost more at first but can be cheaper over time, according to Live Science.

**Replacement head cost.** Replace brush heads about every three months, or sooner if the bristles fray. Official heads for some models are expensive, so factor that into the real cost.

**Apps and fun features.** Apps, characters and lights can motivate reluctant brushers, but reviewers note the novelty can wear off. Treat them as a bonus, not the main reason to buy.

**The ADA Seal.** The ADA Seal of Acceptance means a product has been evaluated for safety and effectiveness. Not every good brush has it, but it's a useful sign.

**Sensory sensitivity.** Some children find vibration or noise uncomfortable. A gentler, quieter brush, or a manual brush, may suit them better.

## Brushing tips for kids

- Brush twice a day for two minutes, usually after breakfast and before bed, as the AAP recommends.
- Use a smear of fluoride toothpaste about the size of a grain of rice for children under 3, and a pea-sized amount for ages 3 to 6.
- Teach kids to spit out the extra toothpaste rather than swallow it, and don't rinse with water right after brushing.
- Help with brushing or check their work until they can do it well on their own, then keep supervising.
- Start regular dental visits early. The AAP suggests a first visit by the first birthday or within six months of the first tooth.
